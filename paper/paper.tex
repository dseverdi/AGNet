\begin{abstract}
The \emph{Art Gallery Problem} (AGP) is a classic NP-hard problem in computational geometry, which involves placing the minimum number of guards in an art gallery (modeled as a polygon) such that the guards together can observe the entire area. In this paper, we present a comparative study of three approaches for solving the AGP: (1) a conventional greedy heuristic, (3) a supervised learning approach using a deep neural network, and (3) a reinforcement learning approach using the REINFORCE algorithm.  We describe the methodological distinctions of these approaches and evaluate their performance on a common set of gallery floorplan instances. Key metrics of interest are the \emph{coverage} (fraction of area covered by the placed guards) and the \emph{relative guard set size} (the size of the guard set relative to the optimal minimum). Our experiments show that the greedy algorithm reliably achieves 100\% coverage with solutions on average only about 17\% larger than the optimum. The supervised learning approach attains high coverage on smaller instances (up to 95\% on average) but often uses significantly more guards (up to 20 times the optimum) and its coverage deteriorates on larger, unseen instances. We analyze the advantages and limitations of each approach and discuss the implications for using machine learning to tackle NP-hard geometric problems like the AGP. The reinforcement learning (RL) approach can learn to place guards covering around 90\% of the area, but with larger variance and typically using about 1.5--3.4 times the optimal number of guards. 
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
The Art Gallery Problem (AGP) is a well-known problem in computational geometry: given a polygonal region (an art gallery) with $n$ vertices, determine the minimum number of guards and their positions such that the entire area of the gallery is observed (visible) by at least one guard. A guard is usually modeled as a point with an unobstructed 360-degree field of view within the polygon. The AGP and its variants (e.g., with different guard visibility ranges or polygon types) have been extensively studied \cite{O'Rourke1987, lee1986} due to their theoretical complexity and practical relevance in areas like surveillance and sensor placement. The decision version of AGP is NP-complete \cite{lee1986}, and computing the optimal minimum guard set is NP-hard, motivating the development of approximation algorithms and heuristics in computational geometry.

Traditional approaches to AGP include exact methods such as integer programming and exhaustive search for small instances, as well as various heuristic and approximation algorithms for larger instances. One classical result is that $\lfloor n/3 \rfloor$ guards are always sufficient for covering a simple polygon of $n$ vertices (the Art Gallery Theorem) \cite{O'Rourke1987}, but finding the minimum required guards for a given layout is much harder. Greedy algorithms have been proposed that iteratively place guards to cover as much uncovered area as possible \cite{GonzalezBanos2001}, typically yielding a feasible (though not always optimal) solution in reasonable time.

Recently, there has been growing interest in applying machine learning techniques to combinatorial optimization problems, including routing, scheduling, and covering problems \cite{Vinyals2015, Bello2016}. Such approaches aim to \emph{learn} heuristics or direct mappings from problem instances to solutions, potentially generalizing to new instances without solving each from scratch. In this paper, we explore two learning-based methods for the AGP: a supervised learning approach and a reinforcement learning approach, and compare them against a conventional greedy heuristic baseline.

The reinforcement learning (RL) approach involves training a model (in our case, a form of \emph{pointer network} \cite{Vinyals2015}) to construct guard sets sequentially. The model is trained with a reward signal that encourages maximizing coverage of the polygon while penalizing excessive use of guards, using the REINFORCE policy gradient algorithm \cite{Williams1992}. The supervised learning approach involves training a deep neural network on example solutions (for smaller gallery instances where near-optimal solutions are known) to directly predict guard positions for new instances. 

Our contribution in this work is a systematic comparison of these three approaches on a common testbed of gallery floorplans. We analyze their performance in terms of coverage achieved and the number of guards used relative to the optimal solution. We also examine the generalization of the learning-based methods to larger or more complex instances beyond their training data. The goal is to understand the trade-offs between traditional algorithmic solutions and modern learning-based solutions for the AGP, providing insight into where machine learning can be beneficial and where it still falls short.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In Section~\ref{sec:methods}, we describe each of the three methods in detail, highlighting the methodological distinctions. Section~\ref{sec:experiments} outlines the experimental setup, including the datasets of polygonal rooms and the evaluation metrics. We then present and discuss the results in Section~\ref{sec:results}, focusing on coverage and solution size comparisons across the methods. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes the paper with a summary of findings and perspectives on future work.


\paragraph{Problem and Notation.} Let $s\subset\mathbb{R}^2$ denote a simple polygonal region (the closed set consisting of its interior and boundary), specified by an ordered vertex sequence $V(s)=(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ with $x_i\in\mathbb{R}^2$. A \emph{guard} is any point $g\in s$ with unobstructed $360^\circ$ visibility inside $s$. For any finite guard set $G\subseteq s$, define the visibility region and coverage
\begin{equation}
\Vis{G}{s} \,=\, \{\, q\in s : \exists g\in G,\ \overline{gq}\subseteq s \,\},
\qquad
\Covf{G}{s} \,=\, \frac{\Area{\Vis{G}{s}}}{\Area{s}}\in[0,1].
\label{eq:vis-cov-general}
\end{equation}
The (point-guard) \emph{Art Gallery Problem (AGP)} asks for a minimum-cardinality guard set $G^*\subseteq s$ such that $\Covf{G^*}{s}=1$. We denote $\OPT=|G^*|$.

In this paper we focus on the \emph{vertex-guard} variant, where guards must be chosen from the polygon's vertex set $V(s)=\{x_1,\dots,x_n\}$. We encode a solution by an index set $\pi\subseteq \{1,\dots,n\}$, corresponding to the guard set $G(\pi)=\{x_i: i\in\pi\}$. The visibility and coverage in \eqref{eq:vis-cov-general} then specialize to
\begin{equation}
\Vis{\pi}{s} \,=\, \{\, q \in s : \exists i \in \pi,\ \overline{x_i q} \subseteq s \,\},
\qquad
\Covf{\pi}{s} \,=\, \frac{\Area{\Vis{\pi}{s}}}{\Area{s}}\in[0,1].
\label{eq:vis-cov}
\end{equation}
Let $\pi^\star$ be an optimal index set for the vertex-guard AGP and note that $|\pi^\star|\ge \OPT$. We report the approximation ratio $\rho=|\pi|/\OPT$ (and, when relevant, the normalized size $|\pi|/n$). In all learned policies, actions index vertices and include a distinguished $\EOS$ token to terminate sequences.


\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}
In this section we describe the algorithmic and learning-based methods evaluated in this work. We first present the classical greedy heuristic used as a strong baseline. We then introduce a neural combinatorial framework for AGP solution construction, including the instance/solution encoding and two training paradigms: supervised learning and reinforcement learning.

\subsection{Greedy heuristic}
The greedy heuristic is a classical, instance-specific algorithm that iteratively selects guard locations to maximize immediate coverage gain. We operate in the vertex-guard model: solutions are index sets $\pi\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}$ and coverage is measured by $\Covf{\pi}{s}$ (see Section~1).

At step $t$ the algorithm has a current index set $\pi_t$. The marginal gain of adding vertex index $i\notin\pi_t$ is
\begin{equation}
\Delta(i\mid \pi_t; s) \,=\, \Covf{\pi_t\cup\{i\}}{s} - \Covf{\pi_t}{s}.
\end{equation}
The greedy choice is
\begin{equation}
i_{t+1} \in \arg\max_{i\in\{1,\dots,n\}\setminus\pi_t} \Delta(i\mid\pi_t; s),
\end{equation}
and $\pi_{t+1}=\pi_t\cup\{i_{t+1}\}$. The procedure terminates when coverage reaches a threshold $\tau_c$ (typically 1), no vertex yields positive marginal gain, or a budget $B$ is reached. Ties are broken deterministically (e.g., by index order).

The greedy method provides a simple, interpretable baseline with guaranteed monotone coverage increase per step; it requires visibility computations for all candidate vertices at every iteration and thus scales polynomially with $n$ depending on the visibility computation cost.

\subsection{Neural combinatorial approach}
We frame learning-based AGP solvers as neural combinatorial models that construct solutions sequentially. Our implementation follows the pointer-network style of sequence generation over the vertex set \cite{Vinyals2015}, with a specialized encoder for geometric input and a decoder that emits vertex indices or a termination ($\EOS$) token.

\subsubsection{Instance and solution encoding}
An instance is the polygonal region $s$ together with its ordered vertex sequence $V(s)=(x_1,\dots,x_n)$. We represent each vertex $x_i$ by its 2D coordinates and (optionally) auxiliary features (e.g., vertex internal angle, local concavity indicators). The encoder maps the set of vertex representations to a latent embedding per vertex; in practice we use a permutation-aware encoder (e.g., an RNN over the vertex order or a graph/transformer-style encoder) so that the decoder can attend over vertex embeddings.

A solution is an index sequence $\pi=(\pi_1,\dots,\pi_T)$ where each token $\pi_t\in\{1,\dots,n,\EOS\}$. The sequence induces the selected index set $\{\pi_t:\pi_t\ne\EOS\}$; repeated indices are idempotent and masking can be used to prevent duplicates when desired.

\paragraph{Pointer network} Denote the encoder's per-vertex embeddings by
$\{h_j\}_{j=1}^n$, computed from $V(s)=(x_1,\dots,x_n)$. The decoder defines an
autoregressive distribution over index sequences by the chain rule
\begin{equation}
p_\theta(\pi\mid s) \,=\, \prod_{t=1}^{T} p_\theta(\pi_t\mid s,\pi_{<t}).
\label{eq:chain-rule}
\end{equation}
At decoding step $t$ the decoder state is $d_t$ and each candidate index
$j\in\{1,\dots,n\}$ is assigned an unnormalized score
\begin{equation}
u^t_j \,=\, v^\top \tanh(W_1 d_t + W_2 h_j), \qquad j=1,\dots,n,
\label{eq:ptr-score}
\end{equation}
and the next-token distribution (including an explicit $\EOS$ choice) is
computed with a softmax with temperature $\tau>0$
\begin{equation}
p_\theta(\pi_t=j\mid s,\pi_{<t}) \,=\, \mathrm{softmax}(u^t/\tau)_j.
\label{eq:ptr-softmax}
\end{equation}
Invalid candidates (already-selected indices, padding) are masked before the
softmax. This $p_\theta(\cdot\mid s)$ serves as the policy used by the
supervised (maximum likelihood) and reinforcement-learning (REINFORCE)
objectives below.

We mask invalid indices and the EOS during decoding, and optionally restrict or penalize repeated selections. The pointer network as a solution sampler can be used either as a direct generator (single rollout) or as a sampler producing multiple candidates later re-ranked by a learned or heuristic evaluator.

Furthermore, we use the notation $\mathcal{D}$ to denote the instance source: either (i) the underlying distribution over polygon instances from which problems are sampled, or (ii) its empirical approximation (the finite training dataset) $\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{train}}=\{s_i\}_{i=1}^N$ used in practice.

\subsubsection{Supervised learning}
In the supervised paradigm we train the model to imitate example solutions (labels). Assuming a target mapping $s\mapsto \pi^*(s)$ (from optimal solutions), the training loss is the expected sequence-level negative log-likelihood:
\begin{equation}
J(\theta) \,=\, \mathbb{E}_{s\sim \mathcal{D}} \big[\, - \log p_\theta(\pi^*(s)\mid s) \,\big].
\end{equation}
unrolled with Eq.~\eqref{eq:chain-rule}. In practice, the expectation is minimized via its empirical average over the labeled training set.
Supervised training requires labeled sequences; for vertex-guard AGP we obtain targets either from exact solvers (for small instances) or from greedy/high-quality heuristics. 

\subsubsection{Reinforcement learning (REINFORCE)}
To directly optimize coverage and guard-count trade-offs we adopt a policy-gradient formulation following \cite{Bello2016}. We use the stochastic policy $p_\theta(\pi\mid s)$ factorized as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:chain-rule}.
 The task is maximizing expected reward. We denote by $R(\pi\mid s)$ the scalar reward assigned to sequence $\pi$ on instance $s$ (higher is better); in practice $R(\pi\mid s)$ combines coverage and guard-usage terms (see Eq.~\eqref{eq:reward-def}). The expected objective is
\begin{equation}
J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mathcal{D}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi\sim p_\theta(\cdot\mid s)}\big[ R(\pi\mid s) \big],
\label{eq:exp-reward}
\end{equation}
Using the log-derivative trick (REINFORCE \cite{Williams1992}), the policy gradient is
\begin{equation}
\nabla_\theta J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{s,\pi}\big[ R(\pi\mid s)\,\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\pi\mid s) \big].
\label{eq:reinforce}
\end{equation}

The Monte Carlo estimator in Eq.~\eqref{eq:reinforce} typically has high variance, which slows learning. A standard variance-reduction trick \cite{Barto} is to subtract any baseline $b(s)$ that does not depend on the sampled sequence $\pi$:
\begin{equation}
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) \,=\, \mathbb{E}_{s,\pi}\big[\, (R(\pi\mid s) - b(s))\, \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\pi\mid s) \,\big].
\end{equation}
 In practice we approximate $b(s)$ with a learned critic $b_\omega(s)$, yielding the empirical actor gradient in Eq.~\eqref{eq:mc-grad} and training the critic with the MSE loss in Eq.~\eqref{eq:critic-loss}.

We use a smooth penalty-as-reward that trades off uncovered area and
relative guard usage:
\begin{equation}
R(\pi\mid s) \,=\, -\Big[\, \big(1-\Covf{\pi}{s}+\eps\big)^{\alpha}\,\Big(\tfrac{|\pi|}{|V(s)|}+\eps\Big)^{p} \, + \, \delta\,\mathbf{1}\{\,1-\Covf{\pi}{s}>\delta_0\,\} \,\Big].
\label{eq:reward-def}
\end{equation}
Here $\eps>0$ is a small smoothing constant to avoid zero gradients/numerical issues at the boundaries (coverage $=1$ or $|\pi|=0$); the exponent $p\ge1$ controls how strongly we penalize using many guards relative to uncovered area (larger $p$ increases the guard-count penalty); and the additive term $\delta\,\mathbf{1}\{1-\Covf{\pi}{s}>\delta_0\}$ imposes a fixed penalty whenever any area remains uncovered (tolerance $\delta_0$), pushing solutions toward full coverage. The exponent $\alpha\ge1$ similarly tunes sensitivity to uncovered area. Typical values: $\alpha,p\in[1,3]$, $\eps\in[10^{-6},10^{-3}]$, $\delta_0\approx10^{-6}$; $\delta$ is set large enough to discourage leaving tiny uncovered pockets.
For training we use a Monte Carlo policy-gradient estimator with a learned state-value baseline (actor–critic). Drawing a batch $\{s_i\}_{i=1}^B$ and sampling one sequence per instance $\pi_i\sim p_\theta(\cdot\mid s_i)$, the gradient is approximated by
\begin{equation}
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) \;\approx\; \frac{1}{B} \sum_{i=1}^B \big( R(\pi_i\mid s_i) - b_\omega(s_i) \big)\, \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\pi_i\mid s_i).
\label{eq:mc-grad}
\end{equation}
Accordingly, the empirical actor loss minimized on the batch is
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta) = -\frac{1}{B} \sum_{i=1}^B \big(R(\pi_i\mid s_i)-b_\omega(s_i)\big) \; \log p_\theta(\pi_i\mid s_i).
\label{eq:emp-loss}
\end{equation}
Note $\log p_\theta(\pi\mid s)=\sum_{t=1}^{T} \log p_\theta(\pi_t\mid s,\pi_{<t})$ by the chain rule, linking this actor loss to the token-level cross-entropy used in supervised learning.
The critic $b_\omega(s)$ is trained to regress the observed returns with a mean-squared error objective
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_v(\omega) \,=\, \frac{1}{B} \sum_{i=1}^B \big( b_\omega(s_i) - R(\pi_i\mid s_i) \big)^2.
\label{eq:critic-loss}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Tabular Q-learning for AGP}
In addition to policy-gradient training, we evaluate a simple tabular Q-learning baseline tailored to the vertex-guard AGP. The agent constructs a guard set by toggling vertex selections, using a compact state abstraction and a cache-accelerated coverage computation.

\paragraph{State, actions, and dynamics.}
Let $\pi_t \subseteq \{1,\dots,n\}$ be the selected index set at step $t$ and let the state be the solution cardinality $s_t = |\pi_t|$. An action $a_t \in \{1,\dots,n\}$ toggles membership of vertex $a_t$ in the set: if $a_t\in\pi_t$ it is removed, otherwise it is added. The next solution is $\pi_{t+1} = \pi_t \triangle \{a_t\}$ (symmetric difference) and the next state is $s_{t+1} = |\pi_{t+1}|$.

\paragraph{Reward.}
After applying $a_t$, the agent receives a scalar reward equal to the attained coverage of the updated solution,
\begin{equation}
  r_t \;=\; \Covf{\pi_{t+1}}{s} \,\in[0,1],
\end{equation}
computed by uniting precomputed per-vertex visibility regions and normalizing by the polygon area. Episodes terminate when coverage reaches a target threshold $\tau_c$ (e.g., 0.90--0.95) or when a step budget $T_{\max}$ is reached.

\paragraph{Learning rule and exploration.}
The agent maintains a tabular action-value function $Q(s,a)$ with Q-learning updates
\begin{equation}
  Q(s_t,a_t) \leftarrow Q(s_t,a_t) + \alpha\,\Big[\, r_t + \gamma\,\max_{a'} Q(s_{t+1},a') - Q(s_t,a_t) \,\Big],
  \label{eq:qlearning-update}
\end{equation}
with learning rate $\alpha \in (0,1]$ and discount $\gamma\in[0,1)$. Action selection is $\eps$-greedy w.r.t. $Q(s,\cdot)$ with an exponential decay schedule $\eps\leftarrow\max(\eps_{\min},\eps\cdot\lambda)$ per episode ($\lambda\in(0,1)$). We track and report the best solution (highest coverage) encountered across steps/episodes.

\paragraph{Implementation notes.}
Visibility regions for all vertices are precomputed once using CGAL (via scikit-geometry) and stored as polygon sets, enabling fast coverage evaluation by set unions. We also memoize coverages for sorted guard sets to avoid recomputation. Default hyperparameters used in our runs mirror the code: $\alpha=0.1$, $\gamma=0.9$, initial $\eps=0.3$ with decay $\lambda=0.995$ to $\eps_{\min}=0.01$, episode budget $T_{\max}\in[15,20]$, and early stopping once the running best coverage consistently exceeds $\tau_c\in[0.90,0.95]$.

\paragraph{Scope and limitations.}
The state abstraction $s_t=|\pi_t|$ is intentionally compact but discards which vertices are selected, so $Q$ values encode action preferences by cardinality rather than instance-specific partial solutions. Despite this limitation, the method serves as a fast heuristic search guided by learned action values and provides a complementary point on the coverage/efficiency/runtime trade-off curve.

\subsection{Learned reward proxy (value\_net) for active search}
Exact polygon-visibility coverage is computationally heavy. For our active-search variant, we therefore employ a learned value network, denoted $f_\phi$, to approximate the reward directly from the instance and a candidate guard set. The proxy is trained to mimic the scalar reward $R(\pi\mid s)$, enabling rapid scoring of multiple candidates per instance.

\paragraph{Definition.}
Given polygon instance $s$ (its vertex coordinates) and a candidate index set $\pi\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}$, the proxy predicts
\begin{equation}
  \hat r_\phi(s,\pi) \;\approx\; R(\pi\mid s),
\end{equation}
where $R(\pi\mid s)$ is the smooth penalty-as-reward used in Eq.~\eqref{eq:reward-def} or its coverage-only variant. The proxy thus serves as a drop-in evaluator for ranking candidates during active search without invoking geometric visibility.

\paragraph{Training.}
We construct supervised pairs $\big((s,\pi),\, r\big)$ by sampling candidate sets and computing the ground-truth reward $r=R(\pi\mid s)$ via visibility. The value network is trained with an $\ell_2$ regression loss
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}(\phi) \,=\, \mathbb{E}_{(s,\pi)}\big[\, \big(\hat r_\phi(s,\pi) - r\big)^2 \,\big].
\end{equation}
At inference, we sample $K$ candidate sequences from the policy and select the best according to $\hat r_\phi$.

\paragraph{Evaluation.}
On a held-out set of 100 instances, the proxy exhibits high fidelity to the true reward with the following aggregate metrics (from \texttt{results/value\_net\_evaluation\_all.json}): MAE=0.0089, RMSE=0.0273, $R^2=0.989$, and $\rho=0.996$. The mean predicted value (0.153) closely matches the mean target (0.149), with similar standard deviations.

\paragraph{Updated dev-set evaluation and insights.}
On a separate dev split comprising 640 samples, the proxy achieves $R^2\!=\!0.516$ and Pearson correlation $\rho\!=\!0.884$, indicating strong ranking fidelity but only moderate variance capture. In scale-free terms, the relative errors are modest: Rel. MAE $\approx$ 8.9% and Rel. RMSE $\approx$ 11.3% of the mean target (866.9). We also observe a small over-prediction bias (+2.3% mean shift; prediction mean 886.9 vs. target mean 866.9) and a higher prediction spread (standard deviation ratio $\sigma_\text{pred}/\sigma_\text{tgt}\approx1.38$). These properties are acceptable for active search where ranking is paramount; in practice, we optionally re-score the top-$k$ candidates with exact visibility to guard against residual bias. Since absolute errors depend on reward scaling, we emphasize scale-independent metrics ($R^2$, $\rho$) and relative errors.

\paragraph{Usage in active search.}
During candidate generation, scoring with $\hat r_\phi$ avoids repeated polygon-visibility calls, substantially reducing per-instance runtime. In practice, one may optionally re-evaluate the top-ranked candidate(s) with exact coverage for verification, but we observed the proxy to be sufficiently accurate for ranking.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}


\subsection*{Evaluation}
We evaluate all three methods on public Art Gallery Problem vertex-guard instances from the AGPLIB library (AGPVG) curated by Couto, de Rezende, and de Souza\footnote{\url{https://www.ic.unicamp.br/~cid/Problem-instances/Art-Gallery/AGPVG/index.html}}. The library groups polygons into several classes and sizes; we focus on the following representative classes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Random simple polygons (non-orthogonal),
    \item Random orthogonal polygons (axis-aligned edges),
    \item Random von Koch (RvK) polygons, and
    \item Complete von Koch (CvK) polygons.
\end{itemize}
Instances are provided as simple polygons in a compact text format: an integer $n$ (number of vertices) followed by a counterclockwise sequence of $n$ vertex coordinates, each written as rational pairs ``int/int''. The full library spans from a few dozen up to several thousand vertices (e.g., 20–2500 in AGP2009a). In our experiments we use a curated subset consistent with our training/evaluation regime: training on up to roughly 100–200 vertices and evaluating within and beyond this range (``smaller'' 10–199 and ``larger'' 200–599 vertices bins reported below). Our splits comprise 8k training instances and 1.2k validation instances.

For each instance, we computed the optimal solution (minimum number of guards for full coverage) using an exact method (formulated as an integer linear program and solved with a solver) for benchmarking purposes. This was feasible for the sizes of instances we considered, albeit computationally expensive; it provided a ground truth for the minimum guard count and an optimal coverage (which is always 100\% by definition of the problem).

We evaluate the following metrics for each method on each test instance:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Coverage}: The fraction of the polygon's area that is covered by the visibility regions of the selected guards. A coverage of 1 (or 100\%) means the entire polygon is observed. Coverage below 1 indicates some blind spots remain. 
    \item \textbf{Guard Set Size}: The number of guards placed by the method.
    \item \textbf{Relative Guard Set Size}: We normalize the guard count by some reference. In this paper, we use two normalizations: (a) relative to the number of vertices (i.e., guard count divided by $n$, the polygon vertex count), to indicate the fraction of vertices used as guards; and (b) relative to the optimal guard count (i.e., guard count divided by the minimum guard count for that instance). The latter ratio directly indicates how close a solution is to optimal in terms of guard count (values $\ge 1$, with 1.0 meaning optimal, 2.0 meaning twice as many guards as necessary, etc).
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Implementation details}

The greedy algorithm was implemented in Python with computational geometry libraries (scikit-geometry Python wrapper of CGAL) for visibility. The RL and supervised models were implemented using PyTorch. We trained both the RL and SL model up to 30 epochs (with various hyperparameter settings as discussed earlier) and evaluated it on a separate validation set. 

We present aggregated results across the test sets. For the smaller instances, all methods are applicable. For the larger instances, we report results for greedy and the supervised model (the RL model was generally trained on instances up to a certain size and did not generalize well beyond that; one could retrain the RL model for larger instances, but we kept training sizes fixed to evaluate generalization).


We assume a guard can stand at any vertex of the polygon (which is a common assumption in art gallery problems, sometimes called the vertex guard problem). Thus, the candidate set of guard positions for each instance is the set of polygon vertices. All methods were constrained to choose guards only at vertices for a fair comparison and to simplify the problem (choosing arbitrary interior points is a more general problem).

\subsection{Discussion of trade-offs}
Synthesizing the above:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Solution quality vs optimal:} Greedy is the most reliable (full coverage, near-optimal count). RL can approach high coverage with promising efficiency signals but with variance; SL lags on both axes.
    \item \textbf{Runtime:} SL is fastest at inference; RL sampling/active search and greedy are moderate; RL Q-learning is slowest. Training cost for learned methods must be amortized.
    \item \textbf{Guarantees and hybrids:} Greedy guarantees coverage; learned methods do not. A practical hybrid is to run a learned policy then greedily top up coverage.
    \item \textbf{Learning difficulty:} The combinatorial/continuous nature of AGP strains current architectures. Stronger geometric encoders and training signals (e.g., explicit count minimization, curricula) are likely needed.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We presented a comparative study of three methods for solving the Art Gallery Problem: a greedy algorithm, a reinforcement learning approach, and a supervised learning approach. Our evaluation on a common set of test polygons revealed that the classical greedy heuristic still outperforms the learning-based methods in terms of guaranteed coverage and efficiency of the solution (guard count). The reinforcement learning method, while promising and able to learn non-trivial strategies, did not consistently achieve full coverage and tended to use more guards than optimal. The supervised learning method, as implemented via a convolutional network heuristic, achieved high coverage on smaller instances but was extremely inefficient in terms of guard usage and failed to generalize well to larger instances.

These results suggest that, for the Art Gallery Problem, current learning-based approaches have not yet matched the reliability and quality of a well-designed algorithmic heuristic for the instances we tested. Nonetheless, the machine learning approaches offer benefits in terms of fast deployment and the potential to improve with more training data or better architectures. They also open the door to tackling variants of the problem where classical methods struggle (for example, when the environment has uncertainty, or the guard visibility has complex constraints, a learned approach might adapt more readily).

In conclusion, greedy and other algorithmic methods remain the go-to solution for obtaining high-quality results in AGP, but reinforcement learning and supervised models are an intriguing alternative that with further development could become competitive. Future work should explore hybrid methods (combining learned components with algorithmic guarantees) and investigate learning strategies that focus on the combinatorial optimality (not just feasibility) of solutions. The Art Gallery Problem continues to be a challenging testbed for both algorithms and learning, and insights from each domain can inform the other.
