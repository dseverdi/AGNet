\title{Learning to Place Guards by Reinforcement:\\
A Geo-Free Neural Policy for the Vertex-Guard\\
Art Gallery Problem}
\author{Domagoj Ševerdija \and Jurica Maltar \and Nathan Chappel \and Domagoj Matijević}
\date{}

\begin{document}

\begin{abstract}
We ask whether a neural policy, trained from a coverage-aware reward over its own rollouts and given no geometric input at inference, can learn to place vertex guards on a simple polygon. The vertex-guard Art Gallery Problem is an NP-hard discrete instance of geometric set cover, and we treat it here as a measurement instrument: restricting guards to polygon vertices yields a finite, pointer-network-natural action space that isolates the geometric-reasoning question from the separate difficulty of continuous optimization, while published instance libraries provide vetted optimal solutions against which approximation ratios can be reported. The reinforcement method we evaluate is a pointer-network policy trained with preference optimization on Bradley--Terry pairs of self-generated rollouts, decoded greedily from coordinates at inference with no visibility matrix and no oracle call. We find that the resulting policy is competitive in guard cardinality on held-out polygons but leaves a non-trivial tail of polygons under-covered, both in distribution and on substantially larger out-of-distribution polygons. To test whether this residual reflects a true limit of the learned representation or a decoder-calibration limit, we train a small per-vertex classifier that reads only the frozen encoder's embeddings and the policy's own seed indicator, controlled by a single scalar threshold; the probe closes the feasibility tail with no geometric input at inference. We read the combined result as evidence that vertex-guard placement is learnable, to a measurable degree, by a reinforcement method that never sees an explicit visibility object, and that the encoder produced by such training already carries substantial geometric structure. We do not claim to compete with classical greedy or local-search heuristics on guard count; the contribution is a bounded answer to what is and is not learnable in this geo-free regime, and an empirical separation of representation quality from decoder quality.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The Art Gallery Problem (AGP) asks for the minimum number of guards needed to observe every point of a simple polygon~\cite{LeeLin1986,ORourke1987AGT}, with applications in surveillance, sensor placement, and robotics~\cite{1545170,DBLP:journals/evi/BighamDK22}. We focus on the \emph{vertex-guard} variant, in which guards must be chosen from the polygon's vertices; the problem remains NP-hard and is a discrete instance of geometric set cover with non-uniform, polygon-dependent visibility regions. The question this paper investigates is whether a neural policy can learn to place such guards from coordinates alone, trained from a coverage-aware reward signal over its own rollouts, with no visibility matrix and no geometric oracle at the moment it chooses guards. We are not asking whether a learned solver can match a classical greedy heuristic on guard count --- it cannot, and we do not claim it does. We are asking whether the underlying combinatorial-geometric task is learnable when the model is denied any explicit geometric input at test time.

The reinforcement method we evaluate is an LSTM pointer-network policy trained with Preference Optimization (PO)~\cite{Pan2025PO} on Bradley--Terry preference pairs~\cite{BradleyTerry1952} drawn from the policy's own rollouts. The policy emits a vertex-index sequence with an $\EOS$ token; the reward couples coverage and guard usage. PO replaces REINFORCE advantages~\cite{Williams1992,Bello2016} with binary preferences between pairs of rollouts, sidestepping REINFORCE's variance and the brittleness of supervised cloning of search labels. We refer to inference under this policy as \emph{geo-free}: at test time the network sees only the polygon's vertex coordinates, with no precomputed visibility matrix, no CGAL output, and no per-vertex area feature.

The policy works, to a degree. On a held-out in-distribution split it reaches mean coverage of roughly $0.97$ at $|S|/\OPT \approx 1.09$, which is competitive in cardinality with classical baselines on these polygons. The honest limit of the result is the tail of the per-polygon coverage distribution: a non-trivial fraction of polygons end up below the feasibility line that one would want any deployed solver to clear, and the failure rate grows on out-of-distribution polygons whose vertex counts exceed the training range. The reinforcement method, by itself, is not feasibility-guaranteed.

To check whether this residual reflects a true limit of the reinforcement-learned representation or only a calibration problem in the decoder, we train a small per-vertex classifier that reads only the frozen encoder embeddings of the policy and predicts a vertex-inclusion probability, controlled by a single scalar threshold. The classifier has no access to visibility information at inference. It closes the feasibility tail: every in-distribution held-out polygon clears the coverage gate, and out-of-distribution failures fall by approximately two orders of magnitude. We read this as direct evidence that the reinforcement-trained encoder already carries enough geometric structure to support a feasibility-guaranteeing decision, and that the policy's coverage tail was a decoder-calibration limit rather than a representation limit. The cost of recovering feasibility is a controlled rise in guard count, which we report explicitly.

Our contribution is twofold. We measure what a reinforcement learner can and cannot extract from a coverage-aware reward on vertex-guard AGP when denied geometric input at inference, and we use a small probe over the frozen encoder to separate the question of representation quality from the question of decoder quality. We do not claim to beat classical greedy or local-search heuristics on guard count; we claim, and document, that vertex-guard placement is learnable to a measurable degree by a reinforcement method that never sees an explicit visibility object.

Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the reinforcement method, what it does and does not absorb, and the representation probe. Section~\ref{sec:experiments} describes the experimental setup. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the results. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} reads the results against the question. Section~\ref{sec:limitations} states limitations and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Problem and Notation}
\label{sec:problem}

Let $s \subset \mathbb{R}^2$ denote a simple polygonal region (the closed set consisting of its interior and boundary), specified by an ordered vertex sequence $V(s) = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$ with $x_i \in \mathbb{R}^2$. A \emph{guard} is any point $g \in s$ with unobstructed $360^\circ$ visibility inside $s$. For any finite guard set $G \subseteq s$, define the visibility region and coverage
\begin{equation}
\Vis{G}{s} \,=\, \{\, q \in s : \exists g \in G,\ \overline{gq} \subseteq s \,\},
\qquad
\Covf{G}{s} \,=\, \frac{\Area{\Vis{G}{s}}}{\Area{s}} \in [0,1].
\label{eq:vis-cov-general}
\end{equation}
The (point-guard) \emph{Art Gallery Problem (AGP)} asks for a minimum-cardinality guard set $G^* \subseteq s$ such that $\Covf{G^*}{s} = 1$. We denote $\OPT = |G^*|$.

In this paper we focus on the \emph{vertex-guard} variant, where guards must be chosen from the polygon's vertex set $V(s) = \{x_1, \dots, x_n\}$. We encode a solution by an index set $\pi \subseteq \{1, \dots, n\}$, corresponding to the guard set $G(\pi) = \{x_i : i \in \pi\}$. The visibility and coverage in~\eqref{eq:vis-cov-general} specialize to
\begin{equation}
\Vis{\pi}{s} \,=\, \{\, q \in s : \exists i \in \pi,\ \overline{x_i q} \subseteq s \,\},
\qquad
\Covf{\pi}{s} \,=\, \frac{\Area{\Vis{\pi}{s}}}{\Area{s}} \in [0,1].
\label{eq:vis-cov}
\end{equation}
Let $\pi^\star$ be an optimal index set for the vertex-guard AGP and note that $|\pi^\star| \geq \OPT$. We report the approximation ratio $\rho = |\pi|/\OPT$, the normalized size $|\pi|/n$, and per-polygon coverage statistics ($\min$, $p_{01}$, $p_{05}$, and the counts $\#\{\Covf{}{} \geq \tau\}$ for $\tau \in \{0.95, 0.99, 0.999, 1\}$).

The general (point-guard) AGP permits guards anywhere in $s$; the vertex-guard variant we work with restricts $G$ to $V(s)$ and so reduces guard placement to a finite discrete choice over the polygon's $n$ vertices --- the natural action space for a pointer-network policy~\cite{Vinyals2015,Bello2016,Kool2019Attention,Kwon2020POMO}, and a setting in which the combinatorial-geometric difficulty of the problem (NP-hardness, non-uniform visibility regions) remains fully present without conflating it with the separate question of continuous optimization in the interior of $s$. Extending to point-guard would require either an action-discretization step or a different policy architecture, and is outside the scope of this paper.

We will say that an inference procedure is \emph{geo-free} if its inputs at test time are limited to the polygon's vertex coordinates and learned features derived from them --- no polygon visibility matrix, no CGAL-computed visibility regions, no per-vertex area or visibility-fraction feature. Geo-free inference does not preclude using exact visibility during evaluation (to report coverage after the fact) or during training (to compute rewards, gate trajectories, or build supervised targets); it restricts what the model reads at the moment it places guards. A solver allowed to query a visibility oracle at every decision step can in principle simulate greedy or local search, and a measurement of what such a solver has learned would be conflated with a measurement of what its oracle can compute. The geo-free constraint isolates the first question from the second, which is what makes ``no explicit geometric knowledge'' a measurable property and not just a rhetorical one.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

This section places the present work in three lines of research: classical algorithms for the Art Gallery Problem, neural combinatorial optimization with reinforcement learning, and supervised post-processing of learned solvers.

\paragraph{The Art Gallery Problem.} Lee and Lin~\cite{LeeLin1986} established NP-hardness for several variants of the AGP; O'Rourke~\cite{ORourke1987AGT} surveys the foundational geometric and combinatorial results. The vertex-guard variant has a long history of approximation algorithms and exact integer-programming approaches; we treat published instance libraries~\cite{art-gallery-instances-page} as the source of polygons in our experiments. Classical greedy heuristics~\cite{GonzalezBanos2001} place guards to maximize marginal coverage and are reliable on small to mid-sized polygons; local search refines candidate guard sets by swapping or removing vertices and is the standard high-quality baseline. We use these classical methods only as evaluation reference points and as a source of supervised labels for the representation probe in Section~\ref{sec:method-arch}; they are not in competition with the reinforcement method on guard count.

\paragraph{Reinforcement learning for combinatorial optimization.} Pointer networks~\cite{Vinyals2015} introduced the architecture of attending over input tokens to produce variable-length output index sequences, and were extended to combinatorial optimization with reinforcement learning by Bello et al.~\cite{Bello2016} using REINFORCE~\cite{Williams1992} with a learned baseline. Subsequent work introduced attention-only architectures for routing problems~\cite{Kool2019Attention} and policy optimization variants such as POMO~\cite{Kwon2020POMO} that exploit problem symmetries. We adopt the more recent preference-optimization (PO) recipe of Pan et al.~\cite{Pan2025PO}, which trains the policy with a Bradley--Terry~\cite{BradleyTerry1952} ranking loss over pairs of solutions sampled from the policy itself, replacing the variance-heavy REINFORCE objective. We treat PO as a reinforcement-style procedure: the loss is computed from policy rollouts on the environment (the polygon), the gradient direction is determined by the reward (coverage versus guard usage), and no supervised labels for the action sequence are used. A broader methodological survey is provided by Bengio et al.~\cite{survey:BENGIO2021405}.

\paragraph{Supervised post-processing of learned solvers.} An orthogonal line of work post-processes a base solver's output with a second model trained by supervised or imitation learning. For combinatorial problems with hard feasibility constraints (such as covering), iterative supervised editors must contend with compounding errors and a train--inference distribution gap; DAgger~\cite{RossGordonBagnell2011DAgger} and its variants partially address the latter. We use a single-shot supervised post-processor (the SetPredictor) not as a competing solver but as a \emph{probe} of the RL-trained encoder's representation. The framing is methodological: if a small classifier reading frozen RL embeddings can recover feasibility, the RL encoder must already carry the relevant geometric information.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

We describe the reinforcement method (Section~\ref{sec:method-pointer}), the diagnostic that explains why a learned \emph{iterative} editor over the same policy does not improve over it (Section~\ref{sec:method-diagnostic}), and the single-shot representation probe (Sections~\ref{sec:method-arch}--\ref{sec:method-inference}).

\subsection{The reinforcement method: PO/BT pointer policy}
\label{sec:method-pointer}

\paragraph{Architecture.} The policy is an LSTM-encoder pointer network with attention-based decoder, of roughly $364$k parameters. A polygon $s$ with vertex sequence $V(s) = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$ is consumed coordinate-wise (no visibility input). The encoder produces per-vertex embeddings $\{h_i\}_{i=1}^n$, $h_i \in \mathbb{R}^{128}$. The decoder attends over the encoder at each step, emits a vertex index or an $\EOS$ token, and stops at $\EOS$ or when the maximum length is reached. Solutions are index sequences $\pi = (\pi_1, \dots, \pi_T)$ over the vertex set $\{1, \dots, n\}$. We collapse $\pi$ to its underlying set when reporting guard counts.

\paragraph{Reward and rollouts.} For each polygon $s$, we sample $K$ rollouts from the policy and score each rollout by a scalar utility $u(\pi \mid s)$ that combines coverage and guard usage; the precise scalarization places coverage above a soft threshold and then prefers smaller guard sets. Coverage during training is computed using a discretized-visibility sampler ($M{=}500$ interior points per polygon) as a cheap approximation; CGAL exact visibility is reserved for evaluation.

\paragraph{Preference-optimization loss.} For each pair $(\pi^+, \pi^-)$ of rollouts with $u(\pi^+) > u(\pi^-)$ above a coverage gate, we apply the Bradley--Terry log-likelihood
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BT}}(\theta) \,=\, -\mathbb{E}_{(s, \pi^+, \pi^-)} \left[\, \log \sigma\!\big( \alpha\, [\, \log p_\theta(\pi^+|s) - \log p_\theta(\pi^-|s)\, ] \big) \,\right],
\label{eq:bt-loss}
\end{equation}
where $\log p_\theta$ is length-normalized over the index sequence (Pan et al.~\cite{Pan2025PO}'s length-control trick, since AGP solutions have variable length). The gradient direction is driven purely by which of the two rollouts achieved a higher reward; no supervised target sequence is used.

REINFORCE with a learned baseline is the classical choice for training pointer policies on combinatorial problems~\cite{Bello2016}, but on the vertex-guard AGP we observed that rewards saturate quickly into a bimodal regime (covered versus uncovered) where REINFORCE advantages collapse and gradient information vanishes. PO sidesteps this: binary preferences over pairs of rollouts retain useful gradient direction even when the rewards themselves become similar, an empirical observation Pan et al.~\cite{Pan2025PO} highlight on routing problems and one we replicate here. At the end of training we are left with a single deterministic policy that maps a polygon's coordinates to a guard sequence in one forward pass; this is the artifact whose properties the rest of the paper investigates, and from this point on its encoder is frozen and treated as a learned feature extractor. Figure~\ref{fig:po_training} reports the held-out training dynamics and shows that the coverage--cost trade-off improves smoothly over the training run rather than collapsing into a degenerate regime.

Right axis: mean approximation ratio $|S|/\OPT$ (or normalized guard count $|S|/n$ if $\OPT$ logging was disabled). The two curves together show that PO/BT improves coverage and guard cardinality jointly rather than trading one for the other, and that the preference-based gradient direction does not collapse as rewards saturate.}{fig:po_training}

\subsection{What the reinforcement method does and does not absorb}
\label{sec:method-diagnostic}

The reinforcement method ends up absorbing two qualitatively different parts of the problem in qualitatively different ways. On the cardinality axis it is broadly successful: on held-out in-distribution polygons (Section~\ref{sec:res-headline}) the policy reaches mean $|S|/\OPT \approx 1.09$, which is competitive with classical heuristics on these sizes, and it does so without ever being shown a visibility object. On the feasibility axis it is less successful: on the same evaluation, $19.3\%$ of polygons end up below $0.95$ coverage with a worst case of $0.830$, and on out-of-distribution polygons the coverage tail blows up to $14.7\%$ of polygons below $0.95$ with a worst case of $0.049$. The reinforcement method has learned to use a small number of vertices, but it has not learned to guarantee that those vertices cover the polygon.

The natural reaction to this is to train a second learned model that applies a sequence of $\textsc{add}/\textsc{remove}/\textsc{stop}$ edits to the policy's seed. We tried three such iterative editor architectures of increasing capacity --- including one with topology features and an auxiliary visibility-prediction loss, and one with DAgger refresh~\cite{RossGordonBagnell2011DAgger} --- and none of them improved over the pretrained policy in any coverage-preserving regime. Three structural reasons make iteration difficult here: rollouts accumulate errors because the editor sees clean trajectories at training but its own evolving predictions at inference; a single classification head over $\{\textsc{add}, \textsc{remove}, \textsc{stop}\} \times \{1, \dots, n\}$ couples action selection with termination, producing $\textsc{stop}$ probabilities that are either too eager or too reluctant; and the training-time and inference-time state distributions diverge in ways that DAgger refresh alone did not close. We mention this only to motivate the single-shot design used for the representation probe: there is no rollout, there is no $\textsc{stop}$ token, and the model is trained and used in the same regime.

\subsection{Probing the representation: a single-shot per-vertex classifier}
\label{sec:method-arch}

The hypothesis under test asks what the reinforcement method has internalized. A natural way to test this is to ask whether a small classifier, conditioned only on the encoder's output and never on a visibility object, can recover the feasibility decision the decoder failed to make. We call this classifier the \emph{SetPredictor}.

For polygon $s$ with vertex sequence $V(s) = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$, the input feature for vertex $i$ is
\begin{equation}
z_i \,=\, [\, h_i \,;\, x_i \,;\, \mathbf{1}\{i \in \pi_{\mathrm{seed}}\} \,] \,\in\, \mathbb{R}^{131},
\label{eq:setpred-feat}
\end{equation}
that is, the \emph{frozen} 128-dimensional encoder embedding $h_i$ from the PO/BT policy, the 2D coordinates $x_i$, and a single binary indicator for whether vertex $i$ is selected by the policy's greedy-decoded seed $\pi_{\mathrm{seed}}$. No visibility matrix, no CGAL output, and no area feature enters the classifier at inference time. The geo-free constraint is preserved.

The architecture is a small Transformer encoder over the $n$ vertex tokens: $3$ self-attention layers, $8$ heads, hidden width $128$, FFN expansion factor $4$, and a sigmoid output head per token. Total parameters: $\sim 464$k. We form a per-vertex representation by concatenating the attention output with two pooled context vectors --- mean-pooled over the seed set and mean-pooled over all valid (non-padded) vertices --- and feed the concatenation to a $2$-layer MLP head:
\begin{equation}
\hat{p}_i \,=\, \sigma\!\big( \mathrm{MLP}([\, v_i \,\|\, S_{\mathrm{pool}} \,\|\, G_{\mathrm{pool}} \,])_i \big),\qquad i = 1, \dots, n.
\label{eq:setpred-arch}
\end{equation}
The output $\hat{p}_i \in [0,1]$ is interpreted as the probability that vertex $i$ should be in the final guard set. Thresholding at $t \in (0,1)$ yields the guard set
\begin{equation}
S(t) \,=\, \{\, i \in \{1, \dots, n\} : \hat{p}_i \geq t \,\}.
\label{eq:setpred-decode}
\end{equation}

The SetPredictor is trained by supervised learning on labels derived from local search over the policy's seed (Section~\ref{sec:method-training}), so it is not itself a reinforcement learner; we use it as a probe of the RL-trained encoder. The only polygon-structural information it reads at inference is the frozen RL embedding $h_i$ together with $x_i$ and the seed indicator, and the logic of the probe is direct: if a small classifier reading only those embeddings can close the feasibility tail, the encoder must already carry the geometric information needed to support that decision.

\subsection{Training the probe}
\label{sec:method-training}

\paragraph{Target sets.} For each training polygon $s$ we obtain a target set $\pi^\star_{\mathrm{LS}}(s)$ by applying local search to the policy's seed solution. The local search uses a coverage gate $\tau = 0.99$ relative to a reference computed with disc-vis sampling ($M = 500$), which is the cheap approximation we use at training time. CGAL exact visibility is used only at evaluation.

\paragraph{Loss.} Binary cross-entropy per vertex with a positive-class weight that automatically balances the empirical guard/non-guard ratio (mean $\approx 5.66$ on the development split),
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(\theta) \,=\, -\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|} \sum_{s \in \mathcal{B}} \frac{1}{|V(s)|} \sum_{i=1}^{|V(s)|} \big[\, w_{+} y_i \log \hat{p}_i + (1 - y_i) \log(1 - \hat{p}_i) \,\big],
\label{eq:setpred-loss}
\end{equation}
where $y_i = \mathbf{1}\{i \in \pi^\star_{\mathrm{LS}}(s)\}$ and $w_{+}$ is the positive-class weight.

\paragraph{Hyperparameters.} $60$ epochs, batch size $32$, learning rate $3 \times 10^{-4}$, AdamW~\cite{Loshchilov2019AdamW} with default weight decay, single random seed ($1234$). Checkpoint selection is on the \texttt{dev\_tune} split (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}).

\subsection{Inference and the threshold knob}
\label{sec:method-inference}

At inference time the procedure is: run the PO/BT policy's encoder on the polygon's coordinates (one forward pass), greedy-decode a seed $\pi_{\mathrm{seed}}$, form the per-vertex features $z_i$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:setpred-feat}, run one forward pass of the SetPredictor to obtain $\{\hat{p}_i\}_{i=1}^n$, and return $S(t)$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:setpred-decode}. No CGAL call, no visibility matrix, no oracle anywhere on this path. The threshold $t$ is the single continuous knob that controls the coverage--size trade-off: lower $t$ admits more vertices, raising both expected coverage and $|S|$, and we report the full Pareto curve over $t \in \{0.20, 0.25, 0.30\}$ in Section~\ref{sec:res-pareto}. One could in principle iterate the SetPredictor by re-running it with the previous prediction's in-set indicator updated, for $K$ passes; we evaluated $K \in \{1, 2, 3, 5\}$ over a sweep of thresholds and found that the predictions are empirically a fixed point after the first pass (Section~\ref{sec:res-fixed-point}). This is the expected behavior of a single-shot model whose input already includes the seed indicator, so we use $K = 1$ throughout.

We work with the splits in Table~\ref{tab:dataset_partition}.

The $70/30$ partition is critical: an earlier alphabetical-name split caused systematic family imbalance between \texttt{dev\_tune} and \texttt{dev\_test} (\texttt{fat-*} polygons concentrated in tune, \texttt{randsimple-*} in test), which produced misleading optimism. The seeded random shuffle removes this bias.

\subsection{Training setup}
\label{sec:exp-training}

The PO/BT reinforcement-trained policy is trained once and frozen; all coverage rewards during PO training use disc-vis sampling. The SetPredictor probe is trained on \texttt{train} only; \texttt{dev\_tune} is used for early stopping and threshold inspection; \texttt{dev\_test}, \texttt{test}, and \texttt{large} are touched only after training and threshold selection are complete. Hyperparameters for the SetPredictor are stated in Section~\ref{sec:method-training}; the policy itself is the existing \texttt{lstm\_bt} checkpoint produced by our PO/BT training script.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:exp-eval}

For each polygon we compute the policy's seed (the policy's own guard sequence, $\EOS$-truncated and greedy-decoded), evaluate it with CGAL exact polygon visibility~\cite{cgal:eb-25b}, and then, separately, run a single SetPredictor forward pass on the policy's encoder output, threshold at $t$ to obtain $S(t)$, and evaluate $S(t)$ with CGAL. We report mean coverage $\Covf{}{}$, mean normalized size $|S|/n$, mean approximation ratio $|S|/\OPT$, and per-polygon coverage distribution (the counts $\#\{\Covf{}{} \geq \tau\}$ for $\tau \in \{0.95, 0.99, 0.999, 1\}$ and the minimum coverage). For binomial counts we report Wilson $95\%$ confidence intervals on the proportion $\#\{\Covf{}{} \geq 0.95\}/n$. The optimal vertex-guard counts $\OPT$ are available for all polygons in \texttt{train}, \texttt{dev}, and \texttt{test} and unavailable for some polygons in \texttt{large}, where we report coverage only.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Read against the question the introduction posed, the reinforcement method does learn to place guards on the polygon, and it does so without ever seeing an explicit visibility object. The geo-free policy reaches mean coverage of roughly $0.969$ at $|S|/\OPT \approx 1.09$ on the held-out in-distribution split, with $296/367$ polygons (Wilson $95\%$ CI $(0.763, 0.844)$) clearing the $0.95$ coverage gate. The cardinality of the guard sets is competitive and the bulk of polygons end up acceptably covered; ``competitive,'' not ``optimal,'' is what the question called for.

The honest counterpart to that finding is the residual tail. Roughly $19.3\%$ of in-distribution polygons and $14.7\%$ of out-of-distribution polygons fall below $0.95$ coverage under the policy alone, and these are not minor near-misses: minimum coverages drop to $0.830$ in-distribution and $0.049$ out-of-distribution. The reinforcement method, by itself, is not a feasibility-guaranteeing procedure, and we read this as the limit of what pure RL delivers in our setup at this scale.

The single most informative measurement in the paper is what happens when we read the encoder rather than the decoder. The SetPredictor probe sees only frozen encoder embeddings and the policy's own seed indicator --- no visibility matrix, no CGAL output, no per-vertex area feature --- and it closes the in-distribution feasibility tail completely ($367/367$ polygons above $0.95$ at $t = 0.20$) and the out-of-distribution tail to within $3$ polygons of $2107$ at the same threshold. There are two reasonable interpretations of this result. One is that the encoder learned enough geometric structure that a small classifier reading its output can recover feasibility on its own; the other is that the decoder's $\EOS$ calibration was the bottleneck and the policy had already identified the right vertices but stopped too early. Both interpretations support the same reading of what the reinforcement method internalized: a representation that already contains more of the relevant geometry than the policy's decoder was able to express. The cost of recovering feasibility through the probe is a controlled rise in guard count, from $|S|/\OPT \approx 1.09$ under the policy to roughly $2.20$ at $t = 0.30$ and $2.90$ at $t = 0.20$, and we report it explicitly rather than disguise it. Figure~\ref{fig:encoder_pca} makes the representation argument visual: the encoder's per-vertex embeddings, projected to two dimensions and colored by guard-membership label, separate without ever having been shown a visibility matrix.

\figincl{gfx/setpred/fig_encoder_pca.pdf}{Two-dimensional projection of the frozen PO/BT encoder's per-vertex embeddings, colored by guard-membership label (LS-derived target). Each point is one vertex of one polygon, pooled across a sample of held-out polygons. Visible separation between guard and non-guard vertices, in a projection that has no access to the visibility matrix, is direct visual evidence that the reinforcement-trained encoder has internalized a guard-relevant geometric signal.}{fig:encoder_pca}

It is worth restating what this paper is not claiming. Classical greedy and local-search heuristics outperform the policy on $|S|/\OPT$ at preserved coverage, and we have not designed the work to contest this; the policy and the probe are not presented as competitors to those methods on guard count. What we are documenting is what is learnable by reinforcement when the model is denied geometric input at the moment of placing guards --- and, jointly, what part of that learning lives in the representation rather than in the decoder. The two formulations of post-hoc improvement we tried bear on the same point. An iterative editor trained over the policy's seed could not improve coverage in any preserved-cardinality regime, and the single-shot probe is empirically a fixed point under iteration; what changes between the two is whether a model is asked to choose a stop time and a sequence of edits or simply to produce a probability per vertex. The single-shot formulation is the one that exposes the encoder's information, and it does so precisely because it removes everything else.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Scope of the ``no geometric knowledge'' claim.} The geo-free constraint is on inference: the policy and the probe see no visibility object at test time. \emph{Training} does see visibility: the PO/BT reward is computed from disc-vis coverage, and the probe's supervised targets are generated by local search using disc-vis as a coverage gate. ``No explicit geometric knowledge'' must be read as ``no geometric oracle at the moment of placing guards.''
  \item \textbf{The probe is supervised, not reinforcement.} The SetPredictor is trained by BCE against local-search-derived labels. It is not itself a reinforcement learner. We position it as a representation probe of the RL-trained encoder, not as an RL contribution.
  \item \textbf{Held-out partition size.} \texttt{dev\_test} has $367$ polygons. Wilson $95\%$ CIs on the coverage-feasibility proportion are reported, but the absolute count is modest. The OOD evaluation on \texttt{test} ($2107$ polygons) provides a much larger second held-out evidence base.
  \item \textbf{Single random seed.} Both the policy and the probe are trained with a single seed ($1234$). Multi-seed runs and seed-variance confidence intervals are left for future work.
  \item \textbf{LSTM, not Transformer, encoder.} The reinforcement-trained policy is an LSTM pointer. A Transformer encoder might produce stronger embeddings and tighten the Pareto curve; we did not retrain.
  \item \textbf{disc-vis approximation in training.} Both the PO/BT coverage reward and the SetPredictor training targets use disc-vis sampling at $M = 500$. Exact CGAL visibility is used only at evaluation.
  \item \textbf{Extreme OOD unaddressed in the main results.} The \texttt{large} split ($n$ up to $2250$) is not evaluated in the main results table. A smoke run on $20$ polygons confirms the probe still tracks the threshold curve; a full evaluation requires further engineering.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have asked whether a neural policy can learn to place vertex guards on a simple polygon from a coverage-aware reward over its own rollouts, with no geometric oracle at the moment it chooses guards, and we have answered the question to a measurable degree. The LSTM pointer-network policy trained by preference optimization on Bradley--Terry pairs reaches mean coverage of roughly $0.969$ at $|S|/\OPT \approx 1.09$ on held-out polygons; a non-trivial fraction of polygons remain under-covered, and the same effect is more pronounced on out-of-distribution polygons whose vertex counts exceed the training range. Reading the encoder rather than the decoder, through a small per-vertex classifier that sees only frozen embeddings and the seed indicator, closes the feasibility tail without any geometric input at inference, and we take this as direct evidence that the reinforcement-trained representation already carries more of the geometry than the policy's own decoder revealed. The cost of recovering feasibility through that probe is a controlled rise in guard count.

We position this work as a measurement of what is learnable in a deliberately constrained setting, not as a competitor to classical solvers on guard count. The reinforcement learner is not the best AGP heuristic; it is a learner that arrived at a usable representation of vertex-guard placement without ever being shown the geometry it had to reason about. Two natural extensions follow. A stronger encoder --- for example, a Transformer trained under the same PO/BT objective --- may shorten the residual coverage tail directly, leaving less work for the probe. A per-instance threshold predicted from the same encoder embeddings would replace the global threshold with an adaptive one and may further close the gap between feasibility and cardinality.
