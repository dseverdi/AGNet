\begin{abstract}
We study neural combinatorial optimization (NCO) for the vertex-guard Art Gallery Problem (AGP), an NP-hard geometric set-cover variant in which guards must be chosen from the vertices of a simple polygon so that their union of visibility regions covers the polygon. A standard LSTM pointer network trained with preference optimization (PO) on Bradley--Terry pairs produces a solver that is fast and often near-optimal in guard count, but exhibits a long left tail in coverage: a non-negligible fraction of polygons are left with uncovered area, and the failure rate grows sharply on out-of-distribution polygons. We diagnose three pathologies of \emph{iterative} neural editors that try to patch the pointer policy --- compounding errors, $\EOS$/action coupling, and a train--inference distribution gap --- and propose a single-shot alternative. The proposed \emph{SetPredictor} reads the frozen pointer encoder's per-vertex embeddings and emits, in one forward pass, an independent inclusion probability per vertex. A scalar threshold $t$ then converts probabilities into a guard set without any geometric input at inference time --- only the polygon vertices and the pretrained pointer encoder are required (``geo-free'' inference). On a held-out partition of 367 in-distribution polygons, lowering $t$ from $0.30$ down to $0.20$ moves the operating point along a Pareto curve: at $t{=}0.20$ the model attains mean coverage $0.9993$, with all 367 polygons reaching coverage $\ge 0.95$ and 363 reaching $\ge 0.99$, while the pretrained pointer alone leaves 71 polygons below $0.95$. On a 2107-polygon out-of-distribution test set (sizes up to $n{=}1000$), the same threshold reduces the pointer's $\#\{\Covf{}{}<0.95\}$ from 310 to 3, a $100\times$ reduction in coverage failures. The cost is a controlled growth in guard count: mean $|S|/\OPT$ rises from $\approx 1.09$ for the pointer alone to $\approx 2.20$ at $t{=}0.30$ and $\approx 2.90$ at $t{=}0.20$ on the held-out set. We position the SetPredictor not as a replacement for classical greedy or local-search heuristics on guard count, but as a distributionally robust safety layer for a neural pointer policy.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The Art Gallery Problem (AGP) asks for the minimum number of guards needed to observe every point of a simple polygon. It is a classical NP-hard problem in computational geometry~\cite{LeeLin1986,ORourke1987AGT} with applications in surveillance, sensor placement, and robotics~\cite{1545170,DBLP:journals/evi/BighamDK22}. We focus on the \emph{vertex-guard} variant, where guards must be selected from the polygon's vertices. The vertex-guard AGP is itself NP-hard and a discrete instance of geometric set cover with non-uniform, polygon-dependent visibility regions.

Neural combinatorial optimization (NCO) has matured into a credible alternative to hand-engineered heuristics for problems such as TSP and CVRP~\cite{Vinyals2015,Bello2016,Kool2019Attention,Kwon2020POMO}. Recent work on \emph{preference optimization} (PO) for NCO~\cite{Pan2025PO} trains a pointer policy with a Bradley--Terry~\cite{BradleyTerry1952} ranking loss over solution pairs, sidestepping the variance of REINFORCE~\cite{Williams1992} and the brittleness of supervised cloning of greedy labels. For the vertex-guard AGP we train an LSTM-encoder pointer network with PO on Bradley--Terry pairs and obtain a fast, coverage-oriented solver that is near-optimal in guard count on in-distribution polygons.

The remaining failure mode is in the tail. A non-negligible fraction of polygons are left with uncovered area --- for example, on a held-out in-distribution set of 367 polygons, 71 of them have coverage below $0.95$ under the pretrained pointer. The failure rate is much higher on out-of-distribution (OOD) polygons whose vertex count exceeds the training range: on a 2107-polygon test split with $n$ up to $1000$, 310 polygons have coverage below $0.95$ and the minimum observed coverage drops to $0.049$. Closing this tail is the central problem of this paper.

A natural approach is to learn a \emph{neural editor} that takes the pointer policy's output and iteratively patches it: add a vertex when coverage is low, remove one when redundancy is high, terminate when a policy says to stop. We tried this. Across three architectures of increasing capacity --- including a 600k-parameter variant with topology features and an auxiliary visibility-prediction loss --- iterative editors failed to improve over the pretrained pointer in any cov-preserving regime. We diagnose three structural pathologies: compounding errors at inference time, $\EOS$/action coupling (a single classification head must decide both \emph{what to do} and \emph{whether to stop}), and a train--inference distribution gap that DAgger refresh alone does not close.

This negative result motivates a single-shot reformulation. The \emph{SetPredictor} reads the pretrained pointer encoder's per-vertex embeddings and predicts, in one forward pass, an independent inclusion probability $p_i \in [0,1]$ for each vertex $i$. A scalar threshold $t$ converts $\{p_i\}$ into a guard set $S = \{i : p_i \ge t\}$. The threshold $t$ is a single continuous knob: lowering it trades guard count for coverage along a smooth Pareto curve. By construction the formulation eliminates all three editor pathologies: there is no rollout, so no compounding errors; there is no $\EOS$, so no action--termination coupling; the model is trained and used the same way, so there is no inference-time distribution gap.

We also make a deliberate methodological choice: the SetPredictor receives only the pointer encoder's embeddings and the vertex coordinates --- \emph{no} visibility matrices, area features, or CGAL-derived geometry at inference time. We call this \emph{geo-free} inference. The encoder embeddings already contain enough geometric information to allow the SetPredictor to recover sets that pass a coverage gate.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Diagnostic.} We exhibit three structural failure modes of iterative neural editors for the vertex-guard AGP (compounding errors, $\EOS$/action coupling, train--inference distribution gap) and show that adding capacity, topology features, an auxiliary visibility loss, and DAgger refresh did not close the gap on a fixed in-distribution evaluation.
  \item \textbf{Method.} We propose SetPredictor, a single-shot per-vertex set-membership predictor that reads pretrained pointer encoder embeddings and emits an inclusion probability per vertex, with inference controlled by a single threshold $t$. The method is geo-free at inference time, requires no rollout, and is trained against a coverage-anchored target set obtained by local-search refinement of the pointer's seed.
  \item \textbf{Empirical.} On a 70/30 held-out partition of the development set (857 / 367 polygons) the SetPredictor at $t{=}0.20$ achieves mean coverage $0.9993$, with $\#\{\Covf{}{}\ge 0.95\} = 367/367$, $\#\{\Covf{}{}\ge 0.99\} = 363/367$, and $\#\{\Covf{}{}=1\} = 243/367$; the pretrained pointer alone leaves 71 polygons below $0.95$. On a 2107-polygon OOD test set (sizes up to $n{=}1000$) the same threshold reduces $\#\{\Covf{}{}<0.95\}$ from 310 to 3, a $100\times$ reduction in coverage failures. Inference is a single forward pass per polygon; the model has $\sim$464k parameters; iterating the prediction is empirically a fixed point.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Problem and Notation}
\label{sec:problem}

Let $s\subset\mathbb{R}^2$ denote a simple polygonal region (the closed set consisting of its interior and boundary), specified by an ordered vertex sequence $V(s)=(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ with $x_i\in\mathbb{R}^2$. A \emph{guard} is any point $g\in s$ with unobstructed $360^\circ$ visibility inside $s$. For any finite guard set $G\subseteq s$, define the visibility region and coverage
\begin{equation}
\Vis{G}{s} \,=\, \{\, q\in s : \exists g\in G,\ \overline{gq}\subseteq s \,\},
\qquad
\Covf{G}{s} \,=\, \frac{\Area{\Vis{G}{s}}}{\Area{s}}\in[0,1].
\label{eq:vis-cov-general}
\end{equation}
The (point-guard) \emph{Art Gallery Problem (AGP)} asks for a minimum-cardinality guard set $G^*\subseteq s$ such that $\Covf{G^*}{s}=1$. We denote $\OPT=|G^*|$.

In this paper we focus on the \emph{vertex-guard} variant, where guards must be chosen from the polygon's vertex set $V(s)=\{x_1,\dots,x_n\}$. We encode a solution by an index set $\pi\subseteq \{1,\dots,n\}$, corresponding to the guard set $G(\pi)=\{x_i: i\in\pi\}$. The visibility and coverage in \eqref{eq:vis-cov-general} then specialize to
\begin{equation}
\Vis{\pi}{s} \,=\, \{\, q \in s : \exists i \in \pi,\ \overline{x_i q} \subseteq s \,\},
\qquad
\Covf{\pi}{s} \,=\, \frac{\Area{\Vis{\pi}{s}}}{\Area{s}}\in[0,1].
\label{eq:vis-cov}
\end{equation}
Let $\pi^\star$ be an optimal index set for the vertex-guard AGP and note that $|\pi^\star|\ge \OPT$. We report the approximation ratio $\rho=|\pi|/\OPT$, the normalized size $|\pi|/n$, and per-polygon coverage statistics ($\min$, $p_{01}$, $p_{05}$, and the counts $\#\{\Covf{}{}\ge \tau\}$ for $\tau\in\{0.95,0.99,0.999,1\}$).

\paragraph{Geo-free inference.} Throughout, we will say that an inference procedure for the vertex-guard AGP is \emph{geo-free} if its inputs at test time are limited to the polygon's vertex coordinates and learned embeddings derived from them --- no polygon visibility matrix, no CGAL-computed visibility regions, no per-vertex area features. Geo-free inference does not preclude using CGAL during \emph{evaluation} (to report exact coverage) or during \emph{training} (to construct target sets). It restricts what the model sees at inference time.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{The Art Gallery Problem.} Lee and Lin~\cite{LeeLin1986} established NP-hardness for several variants of the AGP; O'Rourke~\cite{ORourke1987AGT} surveys the foundational geometric and combinatorial results. The vertex-guard variant has a long history of approximation algorithms and exact integer-programming approaches; we treat published instance libraries~\cite{art-gallery-instances-page} as the source of polygons in our experiments. Classical greedy heuristics~\cite{GonzalezBanos2001} place guards to maximize marginal coverage and are reliable on small to mid-sized polygons; local search refines candidate guard sets by swapping or removing vertices and is the basis for our training-target construction.

\paragraph{Neural combinatorial optimization.} Pointer networks~\cite{Vinyals2015} introduced the architecture of attending over input tokens to produce variable-length output index sequences and were extended to combinatorial optimization with reinforcement learning by Bello et al.~\cite{Bello2016} using REINFORCE~\cite{Williams1992} with a learned baseline. Subsequent work introduced attention-only architectures for routing problems~\cite{Kool2019Attention} and policy optimization variants such as POMO~\cite{Kwon2020POMO} that exploit problem symmetries. We adopt the recent preference-optimization (PO) recipe of Pan et al.~\cite{Pan2025PO}, which trains the pointer policy with a Bradley--Terry~\cite{BradleyTerry1952} ranking loss over pairs of solutions, replacing the variance-heavy REINFORCE objective and the brittleness of supervised cloning. A broader methodological survey is provided by Bengio et al.~\cite{survey:BENGIO2021405}.

\paragraph{Neural editors and post-hoc repair.} An orthogonal line of work post-processes a base solver's output with a learned editor, typically autoregressively. For combinatorial problems with hard feasibility constraints (such as covering), iterative editors must contend with compounding errors and a train--inference distribution gap; behavioral-cloning and DAgger~\cite{RossGordonBagnell2011DAgger} variants are common training recipes. Our work first attempts the iterative-editor route, documents its failure on the vertex-guard AGP, and then proposes a single-shot alternative.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

We start from a pretrained pointer policy (Section~\ref{sec:method-pointer}), document the failure of iterative-editor designs (Section~\ref{sec:method-diagnostic}), and then present the SetPredictor (Sections~\ref{sec:method-arch}--\ref{sec:method-inference}).

\subsection{Pretrained pointer policy (PO/BT)}
\label{sec:method-pointer}

Our base solver is an LSTM-encoder pointer network with attention-based decoder, trained via preference optimization on Bradley--Terry pairs following Pan et al.~\cite{Pan2025PO}. Solutions are index sequences $\pi=(\pi_1,\dots,\pi_T)$ over the vertex set $\{1,\dots,n\}$ with an explicit $\EOS$ token. For a polygon $s$, two candidate solutions $\pi^+, \pi^-$ are compared by a scalar utility $u(\pi\mid s)$ that combines coverage and guard usage; the loss is the standard Bradley--Terry log-likelihood
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BT}}(\theta) \,=\, -\mathbb{E}_{(s,\pi^+,\pi^-)} \left[\, \log \sigma\!\big( \log p_\theta(\pi^+|s) - \log p_\theta(\pi^-|s) \big) \,\right],
\end{equation}
where preferences are sampled from the policy's own rollouts under a coverage gate. The resulting policy is fast, near-optimal in guard count on in-distribution polygons (mean $|\pi|/\OPT \approx 1.21$ on dev), and has a left tail in coverage that motivates Section~\ref{sec:method-diagnostic}.

Throughout the remainder of the paper, \emph{the encoder of this PO-trained pointer is frozen} and used as a feature extractor. We denote its per-vertex embeddings by $\{h_i\}_{i=1}^n$, $h_i \in \mathbb{R}^{128}$.

\subsection{Diagnostic: three failure modes of iterative editors}
\label{sec:method-diagnostic}

We attempted three iterative-editor designs for closing the pointer's coverage tail. Each design takes the pointer's seed solution as input and emits a sequence of edits ($\textsc{add}\ i$, $\textsc{remove}\ i$, $\textsc{stop}$) over the vertices. The three designs differ by capacity and inputs.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Editor v1} ($\sim$80k parameters): per-vertex MLP over coordinates and an in-set indicator; single classification head over $\{\textsc{add}, \textsc{remove}, \textsc{stop}\} \times \{1,\dots,n\}$.
  \item \textbf{Editor v2} ($\sim$600k parameters): adds polygon edge topology features, an auxiliary visibility-prediction loss, and DAgger refresh.
  \item \textbf{Editor v3}: strict coverage gate $\tau{=}0.99$ during training and a strong $\textsc{stop}$ bias.
\end{itemize}

None of the three designs improves over the pretrained pointer in any cov-preserving regime: the median coverage at termination matches the seed, the $|\pi|/\OPT$ does not decrease, and the long left tail in coverage is unchanged. We attribute the failure to three structural pathologies.

\paragraph{(a) Compounding errors.} At training time the editor sees a curriculum of cleanly-labeled edits; at inference time, each edit changes the current set, and a single wrong action takes the rollout into states not seen during training. Mid-rollout error compounds and the policy cannot recover.

\paragraph{(b) $\EOS$/action coupling.} A single classification head over $\{\textsc{add}, \textsc{remove}, \textsc{stop}\}\times\{1,\dots,n\}$ must simultaneously decide \emph{what} to do and \emph{whether} to stop. The two decisions have different statistics: $\textsc{stop}$ should be rare early and common late, while specific add/remove choices depend on the current coverage. The shared softmax produces stop probabilities that are either too eager (the rollout terminates with the seed unchanged) or too reluctant (the rollout cycles between adds and removes without progress).

\paragraph{(c) Train--inference distribution gap.} The editor is trained on trajectories generated from local search applied to the pretrained pointer's seed; at inference it is applied to its own evolving prediction. DAgger refresh closes part of the gap by mixing the model's own rollouts into the training distribution, but in our experiments the closure was insufficient: the model still terminates too early on polygons where the seed is far from a feasible cover.

These three pathologies are structural to iterative editors with a single $\EOS$/action head; they motivate a single-shot reformulation.

\subsection{SetPredictor architecture}
\label{sec:method-arch}

The SetPredictor is a single-shot per-vertex binary classifier that reads the pretrained pointer encoder's embeddings and predicts an inclusion probability per vertex. For polygon $s$ with vertex sequence $V(s)=(x_1,\dots,x_n)$, the input feature for vertex $i$ is
\begin{equation}
z_i \,=\, [\, h_i \,;\, x_i \,;\, \mathbf{1}\{i \in \pi_{\mathrm{seed}}\} \,] \,\in\, \mathbb{R}^{131},
\end{equation}
i.e.\ the frozen 128-dimensional pointer embedding $h_i$, the 2D coordinates $x_i$, and a single binary indicator for whether vertex $i$ is selected by the pretrained pointer's seed solution $\pi_{\mathrm{seed}}$. No visibility matrix, no CGAL output, and no area feature enters the SetPredictor at inference time.

The architecture is a small Transformer encoder over the $n$ vertex tokens: 3 self-attention layers, 8 heads, hidden width 128, FFN expansion 4, and a sigmoid output head per token. Total parameters: $\sim$464k.

\begin{equation}
\hat{p}_i \,=\, \sigma\!\big( \mathrm{MLP}(\mathrm{Att}_3(\mathrm{Att}_2(\mathrm{Att}_1(z_1,\dots,z_n))))_i \big),\qquad i=1,\dots,n.
\end{equation}

The output $\hat{p}_i \in [0,1]$ is interpreted as the probability that vertex $i$ should be a guard. Thresholding at $t\in(0,1)$ yields the guard set
\begin{equation}
S(t) \,=\, \{\, i \in \{1,\dots,n\} : \hat{p}_i \ge t \,\}.
\label{eq:setpred-decode}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Training}
\label{sec:method-training}

\paragraph{Target sets.} For each training polygon $s$ we obtain a target set $\pi^\star_{\mathrm{LS}}(s)$ by applying local search to the pretrained pointer's seed solution. The local search uses a relative coverage gate $\tau{=}0.99$ relative to a reference computed with disc-vis sampling ($M{=}500$), which is the computationally cheap approximation we use at training time. CGAL exact visibility is used only at evaluation.

\paragraph{Loss.} Binary cross-entropy per vertex with a positive-class weight that automatically balances the empirically observed ratio of guards to non-guards (mean $\approx 5.66$ on dev),
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(\theta) \,=\, -\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|} \sum_{s\in\mathcal{B}} \frac{1}{|V(s)|} \sum_{i=1}^{|V(s)|} \big[\, w_{+} y_i \log \hat{p}_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-\hat{p}_i) \,\big],
\end{equation}
where $y_i = \mathbf{1}\{i \in \pi^\star_{\mathrm{LS}}(s)\}$ and $w_{+}$ is the positive-class weight.

\paragraph{Hyperparameters.} 60 epochs, batch size 32, learning rate $3\times 10^{-4}$, AdamW~\cite{Loshchilov2019AdamW} with default weight decay, single random seed (1234). Checkpoint selection is on \texttt{dev\_tune} (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}).

\subsection{Inference and threshold control}
\label{sec:method-inference}

At inference time the model receives a polygon $s$, computes the pretrained pointer's seed solution $\pi_{\mathrm{seed}}$ (a single rollout), forms vertex features $z_i$, runs one forward pass of the SetPredictor to obtain $\{\hat{p}_i\}_{i=1}^n$, and returns the guard set $S(t)$ defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:setpred-decode}.

\paragraph{Threshold as a Pareto knob.} The threshold $t$ is the single continuous knob that controls the coverage--size trade-off. Lower $t$ admits more vertices into $S$, raising both expected coverage and $|S|$. We report the full Pareto curve over $t\in\{0.20, 0.25, 0.30\}$ in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\paragraph{Iterative inference is a fixed point.} One might consider iterating the SetPredictor: re-run it with the previous prediction's in-set indicator updated, for $K$ passes. We evaluated $K\in\{1,2,3,5\}$ over a sweep of thresholds; the predictions are empirically a fixed point after the first pass. This is the expected behavior of a single-shot model whose input already includes the seed indicator: there is no meaningful additional signal to extract in subsequent passes. We use $K{=}1$ throughout.

\subsection{Training setup}
\label{sec:exp-training}

The PO/BT pretrained pointer is trained once and frozen. The SetPredictor is trained on \texttt{train} only; \texttt{dev\_tune} is used for early-stopping and threshold inspection; \texttt{dev\_test}, \texttt{test}, and \texttt{large} are touched only after training and threshold selection are complete. We train for 60 epochs with batch size 32, learning rate $3\times 10^{-4}$, AdamW with default weight decay, and a single random seed (1234).

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:exp-eval}

For each polygon we compute the pretrained pointer's seed, run a single SetPredictor forward pass, threshold at $t$ to obtain $S(t)$, and evaluate $S(t)$ with CGAL exact polygon visibility~\cite{cgal:eb-25b}. We report mean coverage $\Covf{}{}$, mean normalized size $|S|/n$, mean approximation ratio $|S|/\OPT$, and per-polygon coverage distribution (the counts $\#\{\Covf{}{}\ge\tau\}$ for $\tau\in\{0.95, 0.99, 0.999, 1\}$ and the minimum coverage). For binomial counts we report Wilson 95\% confidence intervals on the proportion $\#\{\Covf{}{}\ge 0.95\}/n$.

Optimal guard counts $\OPT$ are taken from the AGPVG library's optimal solutions, available for all polygons in \texttt{train}, \texttt{dev}, and \texttt{test}; they are not available for all polygons in \texttt{large}, where we report coverage only.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{The SetPredictor is a safety layer, not a guard-count winner.} Classical greedy and local search beat the SetPredictor on $|S|/\OPT$ at preserved coverage --- this is not in dispute and we do not claim otherwise. The SetPredictor's role is different: it is the first method we know of that closes the pretrained pointer's coverage tail in a single forward pass at inference time, with no geometric input. The relevant comparison is therefore not against an oracle solver but against the pretrained pointer it post-processes.

\paragraph{Why does a single-shot model work where iterative editors fail?} Three reasons, each tied to a pathology of Section~\ref{sec:method-diagnostic}. First, there are no rollouts, so compounding errors cannot occur. Second, there is no $\EOS$ head: the model decides only what is in or out, never when to stop. Third, the model is trained and used the same way --- it receives the seed indicator at training and at inference --- so the train--inference distribution gap closes by construction.

\paragraph{Why does geo-free inference suffice?} The pretrained pointer encoder's per-vertex embeddings $h_i$ were trained on a coverage-aware preference objective and therefore carry geometric information about each vertex's role in the polygon. The SetPredictor reuses this information rather than recomputing it. This explains why a small ($\sim$464k) Transformer over the encoder embeddings is enough; the heavy geometric lifting was done once, by the pointer.

\paragraph{The threshold is the right interface.} The single-scalar threshold $t$ is a continuous and predictable knob: lower $t$ uniformly increases both coverage and $|S|$ along a Pareto curve, and the curve is monotone in our experiments. This makes the SetPredictor easy to deploy with an explicit coverage budget: choose $t$ such that the empirical $\#\{\Covf{}{}\ge\tau\}/n$ on a validation set meets the desired target.

\paragraph{Out-of-distribution generalization is the dominant effect.} The $100\times$ reduction in coverage failures on \texttt{test} is the most consequential result of the paper. The pretrained pointer collapses on polygons whose vertex count exceeds its training range; the SetPredictor, despite being trained on the same range, generalizes to much larger polygons because the per-vertex prediction depends only on local features and an embedding that is in turn computed locally (per vertex). The architecture does not have to extrapolate over sequence length the way an autoregressive policy does.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Held-out partition size.} \texttt{dev\_test} has 367 polygons. Wilson 95\% CIs on the coverage-feasibility proportion are reported in Table~\ref{tab:headline}, but the absolute count is modest. We mitigate by reporting the much larger OOD evaluation (\texttt{test}, 2107 polygons).
  \item \textbf{Single random seed.} Both the pointer and the SetPredictor are trained with a single seed (1234). Multi-seed training and Wilson CIs over seed variance are left for future work.
  \item \textbf{Alphabetical-split caveat.} An earlier alphabetical partition of \texttt{dev} produced misleadingly optimistic headline numbers. The seeded random shuffle we use closes this leak, but readers of prior internal notes should be aware that the in-distribution headline moved when we fixed the split.
  \item \textbf{LSTM, not Transformer, encoder.} The pretrained pointer is an LSTM. A Transformer-encoder pointer might produce stronger embeddings and tighten the Pareto curve; we did not retrain.
  \item \textbf{disc-vis approximation at training time.} The target sets used to train the SetPredictor are obtained by local search under disc-vis sampling ($M{=}500$), an approximation. Exact CGAL visibility is used only at evaluation. Training under exact visibility is computationally expensive and is left for future work.
  \item \textbf{Extreme OOD is unaddressed.} The \texttt{large} split ($n$ up to $2250$) is not evaluated in the main results table; a smoke run on 20 polygons shows the predictor still tracks the threshold curve, but a full evaluation requires further engineering and is deferred.
  \item \textbf{Optimal guard counts are taken from the library.} We use the AGPVG library's optimal solutions to compute $|S|/\OPT$; we did not re-verify these with an independent ILP solver.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We posed the question of how to close the coverage tail of a PO/BT-trained pointer policy for the vertex-guard Art Gallery Problem. We showed that iterative neural editors, despite increasing capacity, topology features, an auxiliary visibility loss, and DAgger refresh, fail to improve over the pointer in any cov-preserving regime, and traced the failure to three structural pathologies (compounding errors, $\EOS$/action coupling, train--inference distribution gap). We then introduced the SetPredictor, a single-shot per-vertex set-membership predictor over frozen pointer embeddings, geo-free at inference time and controlled by a single scalar threshold. On 367 held-out in-distribution polygons, the SetPredictor eliminates the pointer's sub-$0.95$ coverage tail entirely; on 2107 out-of-distribution polygons it reduces coverage failures by two orders of magnitude. The cost is a controlled and tunable growth in guard count.

We position the SetPredictor as a distributional safety layer for neural pointer solvers, not as a competitor to classical greedy or local search on guard count. Two directions suggest themselves. The first is to retrain the pretrained pointer with a Transformer encoder and a coverage-aware preference signal that already keeps the tail short; the SetPredictor would then need a smaller correction. The second is to study how the threshold $t$ should depend on the polygon (per-instance $t$) rather than being a global constant; the present global-$t$ design is intentionally simple and already adequate.
